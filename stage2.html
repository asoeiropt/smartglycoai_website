Stage 2: User and task analysis

Objective: Identification and characterization of the target users, tasks and scenarios.

1

Overview & Problem Statement

People with type 1 diabetes do not produce insulin, meaning that many of the decisions a healthy body makes automatically have to be made by the patient, several times a day, every day. Our analysis identified four key friction areas:

⚖️ Counting Carbs & Doses

Estimating portion sizes and mixed ingredients by eye is difficult. A small error leads to a wrong dose. Doing this calculation 3-5 times a day, often in public, is tiring and stressful, leading patients to guess or eat "safe" meals.

📉 Risk of Hypoglycemia

Too much insulin or physical activity can cause dangerous lows. Patients often notice a low only when symptoms appear (e.g., driving or sleeping). They must constantly check sensors and interpret trends themselves.

🧩 Scattered Information

Data is spread across CGM apps, memory, phone notes, and paper settings. It is hard for patients to understand how food, insulin, and activity correlate over time, and mistakes in past records are rarely corrected.

👨‍⚕️ Healthcare Communication

Endocrinologists only see patients every few months for 15-20 minutes. Relying on incomplete patient memory to adjust treatments means appointment time is spent collecting data instead of making decisions.

Our Proposal

SmartGlycoAI is a mobile app that brings these tasks together. It shows current CGM trends, estimates carbs from photos, suggests insulin doses, warns of predicted lows, and keeps an editable diary. Healthcare professionals access this shared data via a web platform to follow patients between appointments.

3

Tasks Analysis

Task 1: Log a meal and get insulin recommendation (Patient)

Objective: Accurately log food intake using computer vision to receive an insulin dose suggestion.
Pre-conditions: User is logged in, treatment settings (ICR, ISF, target) are filled, CGM is connected.
Time/Freq: 3-5 times a day (before meals), under 1 minute.
Exceptions: Camera fails to recognize food; CGM sensor loses Bluetooth connection.
Sub-tasks:
  1. Open the meal logging module.
  2. Take a photo of the meal.
  3. Confirm or adjust the AI's carbohydrate estimation.
  4. Review and accept/reject the suggested insulin dose.

Task 2: Review patient's glycemic history (Doctor/Nurse)

Objective: Analyze weekly/monthly glucose trends to adjust medical treatment.
Pre-conditions: Patient shared data; professional logged into the web version of the platform.
Time/Freq: Before/during appointments (every few months), occasionally between appts.
Exceptions: Patient has not synced their data recently.
Sub-tasks:
  1. Access the specific patient's profile.
  2. Filter data by the last 30 days.
  3. Identify patterns of hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia.

Task 3: Respond to a predictive low-glucose alert (Patient)

Objective: Act before a hypoglycemia happens, after the app predicts a fast drop in glucose.
Pre-conditions: CGM sensor is connected and notifications are allowed.
Time/Freq: Few times a week. Urgent: user has about 20 minutes to act.
Exceptions: User misses notification; false alarm from sensor; user ignores by mistake.
Sub-tasks:
  1. Receive the alert notification with the predicted value and time.
  2. Open the alert and read the suggestion (e.g. eat 15 g of fast-acting carbs).
  3. Eat the suggested carbohydrates.
  4. Register the correction in the diary, or ignore the alert if not needed.

Task 4: Review and edit the diary history (Patient)

Objective: Check past meals and corrections, and fix any entry with wrong values.
Pre-conditions: User is logged in and has at least one entry in the diary.
Time/Freq: Once a day or a few times a week. About 1 to 2 minutes.
Exceptions: Deletes entry by mistake; enters impossible value; leaves without saving.
Sub-tasks:
  1. Open the Diary tab.
  2. Scroll through the list of meals and corrections.
  3. Open an entry to see its details (photo, time, carbs, insulin dose).
  4. Change the time, description, carbs or insulin dose.
  5. Save the changes, or delete the entry.
4

Scenarios

Task 1

Scenario 1: Lunch at a restaurant

João Antunes is a young adult with type 1 diabetes who uses a FreeStyle Libre sensor. On Tuesday at 1 p.m. he is having lunch with his friends at a restaurant and orders steak with rice and salad. When the food arrives, he opens SmartGlycoAI and sees that his glucose is 115 mg/dL and stable. He taps "Analyse meal" and takes a photo of his plate. The app identifies "steak with rice and salad", estimates 45 g of carbohydrates and suggests 3.0 units of insulin, based on his ratio of 1 unit per 15 g. João thinks the rice portion is bigger than usual, so he changes the carbohydrates to 60 g and the suggested dose is updated to 4.0 units. He takes his insulin, confirms, and the meal is saved in his diary. It takes less than a minute and his friends barely notice.

Tasks 3 & 4

Scenario 2: A low on the way home

At 6 p.m. João is on the bus home after the gym. His phone vibrates with an alert: the app predicts his glucose will reach 65 mg/dL in 20 minutes and suggests eating 15 g of fast-acting carbohydrates. He opens the alert, drinks a small pack of juice he always carries, and taps "Register correction". Later that evening, at home, he opens the Diary to check his day. He remembers that at lunch he left part of the rice on the plate, so he ate fewer carbohydrates than the 60 g he registered, which may explain the low in the afternoon. He opens the lunch entry, changes the carbohydrates to 45 g so that the diary shows what he really ate, keeps the 4.0 units he actually took, and saves the changes.

Task 2

Scenario 3: Preparing an appointment

Dr. Joana Silvia is an endocrinologist at a hospital in Almada, and João has an appointment with her on Thursday. Before the appointment, she logs in to the web version of SmartGlycoAI on her office computer, opens João's profile and filters the data to the last 30 days. She notices that João had several low-glucose alerts in the late afternoon, always on days he went to the gym, and that his glucose is often high after lunch. During the appointment they look at the data together. They decide to change his insulin-to-carb ratio from 1 unit per 15 g to 1 unit per 12 g, and she advises him to eat a small snack before going to the gym. João updates his ratio in the Profile tab. In the following weeks, Martim Garcia, the nurse who follows João, checks his glucose readings to see how he is adapting to the new settings and whether he needs additional support.

5

Interviews & Results

💡 Summary of Results & Design Impact

  1. Quick access to current glucose: João wants to see his glucose/graph in the first few seconds (suggested a widget). Design impact: Current value will be the largest element on the home screen. A widget will be considered later.
  2. Photo carb counting accepted if discreet: Camera use in restaurants is fine if fast. Design impact: Confirms Task 1 as primary; must take under a minute with minimal steps.
  3. AI should support, not replace, decisions: Both João and Dr. Silvia insisted AI advice must be validated. Design impact: Users can always adjust carbs/doses before saving; the app must explain how algorithms work.
  4. Professionals need data in one place: Dr. Silvia wants to analyze glucose, insulin, and progress on a PC. Design impact: Creation of a Web Dashboard with graphs and concerning pattern alerts (Task 2).
  5. Reminders are important: Reminders for checking glucose and taking insulin. Design impact: Will be considered for future project stages.
  6. Interface must be simple (especially for older users): Nurse Martim highlighted issues with medical terms and complicated UI. Design impact: Use large readable values, simple graphs, explain terms (ICR/ISF) in the Profile, and allow configurable notifications (Task 3).

Interview 1: The Patient
João Antunes (Young Adult with Type 1 Diabetes)
Interviewer:
What is the biggest challenge you face in the daily management of your diabetes?
João Antunes:
My biggest challenge is remembering to check my glucose levels, take my medication, and maintain healthy eating habits every day. It's a continuous process that requires a lot of attention before every meal.
Interviewer:
How could our SmartGlycoAI mobile app facilitate this management, especially on a busy day?
João Antunes:
It would help immensely to organize my daily routine, save my glucose measurements, and remind me when I need to take my medication. If I open the app in an emergency, I need to see my current glucose level and the graph for the last few days right in the first few seconds. Having a widget on the phone's home screen would be perfect to see the blood glucose without even opening the app.
Interviewer:
What specific features would you like the application to have, particularly regarding food?
João Antunes:
I would like to have a glucose tracker, medication reminders, a meal log, progress graphs, and simple explanations about diabetes. Taking a photo of the plate for the app to calculate the carbohydrates would be very practical. I would feel comfortable using the camera in a restaurant, as long as the process was fast and discreet.
Interviewer:
Would you like the application to provide personalized recommendations using Artificial Intelligence?
João Antunes:
Yes, especially recommendations about food, exercise, and daily habits based on my glucose history. However, I would want the medical advice to be checked and validated by my healthcare team.
Interviewer:
What would make you use the application every day?
João Antunes:
A simple interface, a quick glucose log, useful reminders, and the ability to clearly see if my condition is improving.
👩‍⚕️
Interview 2: The Doctor
Dr. Joana Silvia (Endocrinologist)
Interviewer:
In what way could an application like SmartGlycoAI help you monitor your patients with diabetes?
Dr. Joana Silvia:
It would help me access patients' glucose measurements, medication history, and health progress in a single place, which would make consultations much more efficient.
Interviewer:
What features would be most important for your clinical work?
Dr. Joana Silvia:
I would prioritize glucose history graphs, alerts for concerning patterns, organized patient records, and the ability to review medication adherence. The fact that the platform has a web version allows me to analyze this data comfortably on my office computer screen.
Interviewer:
How could the application improve communication with your patients?
Dr. Joana Silvia:
Patients could share their glucose measurements and symptoms between consultations, allowing me to better understand their condition and the impact of meals throughout the week.
Interviewer:
Do you consider that AI-generated health recommendations, such as suggesting insulin doses, would be useful?
Dr. Joana Silvia:
Yes, particularly for identifying trends and highlighting potential problems. However, AI should support medical decisions rather than replace professional judgment.
Interviewer:
What would make you trust this application for clinical use?
Dr. Joana Silvia:
Accurate information, secure patient records, clear explanations about the insights generated by AI, and reliable clinical data. A transparent section about the development team and how the algorithm was trained also provides greater security.
👨‍⚕️
Interview 3: The Nurse
Enf. Martim Garcia (Nurse)
Interviewer:
How could this application help you in your daily follow-up work?
Enf. Martim Garcia:
It could help me monitor patients' glucose readings, check if they are following their care routines, and easily identify those who need additional support.
Interviewer:
What features would be most useful to ensure the success of the treatment?
Enf. Martim Garcia:
Medication reminders, tracking patient progress, notifications about missed glucose measurements, and educational information.
Interviewer:
How could the application help patients manage diabetes more independently?
Enf. Martim Garcia:
It could remind them to monitor their glucose, follow their prescribed medication schedules, and maintain healthy habits.
Interviewer:
What problems do you think patients might face when using the application, and how would you solve them?
Enf. Martim Garcia:
Some patients might have difficulties understanding medical information or navigating complicated interfaces, particularly older users. The inclusion of a glossary for technical terms would be an excellent help.
Interviewer:
What would you improve in the design of a diabetes management application?
Enf. Martim Garcia:
I would make the application very easy to use, with large buttons, clear instructions, simple graphs, and notifications that patients can customize. Notifications on the phone should be configurable, such as a subtle vibration, so they are not intrusive in the workplace.
